Title Patterns

Every winner is a plain-English question about a mundane daily activity, projected onto ancient humans. The activity is something the viewer did today (work, rain, drink, travel, winter, clothes). No numbers, no “Top 10”, no clickbait adjectives except one CAPS word occasionally (“TERRIFYING”).

02

Thumbnail Analysis

A locked, instantly recognizable recipe — the same stick-figure cartoon world every time, so the feed reads as a series:

  • Art style: hand-drawn cartoon, round white “stick-figure” heads with dot/line eyes, leopard-print hide wrap, thick black outlines, flat colour with soft cel shading. Zero photorealism. Same characters every video (recurring cast = brand).
  • Composition: 1–5 characters, one clear focal scene (around a campfire, in a cave, walking in desert), wide shot, scene fills the frame. A campfire appears in 7 of 10 thumbnails.
  • Emotion: exaggerated but simple — bliss (NO JOBS), boredom (RAINED ALL WEEK), drunk flush (DRINKS ALL DAY), stoned half-eyes, manic grin with blood (BORN TO KILL?). The face IS the punchline.
  • Text treatment: 2–4 word ALL-CAPS fragment, never the title. Chunky rounded font, yellow fill + black outline (7/10) or white fill + black outline (3/10). Placed top-centre or top-left; occasionally a black arrow pointing at the subject.
  • Palette: warm earth tones (tan, orange fire, brown hide) against a single sky/ground colour (blue sky, grey rain, white snow, purple dusk). High contrast, few colours, reads at 120px.
  • Hook-text logic: either the punchline (“NO JOBS”, “DRINKS ALL DAY”, “STONED ALL DAY”) or the question (“WHY US?”, “TRAVEL HOW?”, “COZY INSIDE?”). Ideal: 2 words, one relatable modern concept.
03

Hook Structures (first 60 seconds)

The top video's cold open is a textbook “You → Them → Doubt → Mystery” hook, and the titles/thumbnails of the other winners follow the same relatable-contrast logic:

  1. 0:00–0:15 — YOU, today (second person, mundane pain): “This morning, a machine screamed at you until you woke up… your day was already decided.” Starts in the viewer's life, never with the topic.
  2. 0:15–0:28 — Hard cut to THEM: “Now, rewind 50,000 years. A man wakes up… no alarm, no schedule… and asks himself a question you have never once been free enough to ask: What should I do today?”
  3. 0:28–0:45 — Pre-empt the objection: “Now, your brain is already fighting this. It's telling you no, ancient life was brutal, short and desperate… That was the official story. But the proof that it's wrong was lying buried in the ground the entire time.”
  4. 0:45–1:05 — Stack the mystery as 3 questions, then promise the route: “How does a man with no technology end up with more free time than you? What did he actually do all day? And where did all of your time go? To answer that, we have to start in the ground.”
“You were never the fastest thing out there. You were the only thing that never has to stop.”— signature aphorism style: short, parallel, quotable; one per segment

Reusable hook template: [Second-person snapshot of a modern annoyance] → [“Now rewind N years.” Same moment, ancient human, freer/stranger] → [“Your brain is already fighting this” — name the textbook belief] → [“But the proof was…” — tease evidence] → [3 stacked questions] → [“To answer that, we have to start with…”].

04

Script Format

Voice: conversational second person (“you”), present tense, short declaratives. Contractions everywhere. No jargon without immediate plain-English gloss. Narrator is a confident friend, not a professor.

Pace: ~225 words/min (2,516 words in 11:12) — fast, almost no pauses; energy carried by sentence rhythm, not by shouting.

Evidence style: every claim is anchored to a named scientist + year + place (“In 1963, an anthropologist named Richard Lee walked into the Kalahari…”, “Blombos Cave, 70,000 years old”, “2008, Germany, vulture-bone flute”, “Polly Wiessner, 2014, 81% of firelight talk was stories”). Sources are listed in the description — trust is part of the brand.

Retention devices (recurring):

  • Guess-the-answer: “So which one is which? Take a guess.” → reveal the opposite.
  • Pre-empted doubt: “You're probably thinking this sounds too good to be real. So let's deal with that.”
  • “But here's the part nobody tells you” pivot into the next segment.
  • Day-in-the-life immersion: “Let me walk you through one. You wake up at dawn…”
  • Number + modern comparison: “17 hours a week… you'll cross 17 hours by Wednesday afternoon.”
  • Callback: the two skeletons from minute 1 return at minute 9.

CTA: none in-script — no “like and subscribe”, no sponsor. Ends on the emotional line.

Arc shape: Myth → Flip → Immerse → Prove ×3 (escalating from body → number → meaning → night) → Villain origin → Mirror back to the viewer. New evidence or reveal every ~75–90 seconds. Segments are stitched with pivot lines, never chapter titles.

06

What Makes It Work — the one replicable mechanism

“Your ordinary day, 50,000 years ago.” Every hit takes one mundane, universal modern experience (work, rain, booze, travel, cold, clothes) and asks how humans handled it before civilisation — then uses real archaeology to flip the viewer's assumption and quietly make them question their own life.

  • Relatability × scale: the hook is about you today; the payoff is about all humans ever. That contrast is the click AND the share.
  • The flip: the viewer's assumption (“ancient life was miserable”) is stated, then overturned with named evidence. Titles that don't contain a flip (Guns, Baby animals) underperform.
  • Series consistency: same stick-figure cast, same campfire, same yellow CAPS text → the feed looks like a show; binge behaviour follows (the channel doubled subs in a month off one hit).
  • Credibility as differentiation: named researchers, years, DOIs in the description — in a niche full of AI slop, cited sources make it feel like TED-Ed at 5× the informality.
  • Emotional exit: no CTA — the last 60 seconds turn the science into a mirror about the viewer's own life. That is what drives comments and shares.
Cloneable formula

[Mundane modern activity] + [ancient humans] → second-person cold open → named-evidence flip → day-in-the-life immersion → 3–4 escalating proofs (each with scientist + year + number) → the “villain” that changed things → mirror ending. 8–11 min, ~225 wpm, stick-figure cartoon visuals, 2–3-word yellow CAPS thumbnail text.
